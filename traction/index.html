A visual field guide / Entrepreneurial Operating System

TRACTION.

Clear direction. Right people. Weekly discipline. Problems solved. Explore the six EOS components, then put the tools to work.

Gino Wickman / Adapted from Brent Pattison's visual reading summary
The premise: Most growing companies experience the same frustrations: lack of control, people challenges, insufficient profit, hitting the ceiling, and the feeling that nothing is working. EOS replaces fragmented efforts with a single operating system. This resource follows the structure and terminology of the original visual summary.

The operating system at a glance

A shared vision only becomes traction when the organization aligns its people, numbers, issue-solving, processes and execution rhythm.

Five recurring frustrations

Lack of control · People · Profit · The ceiling · Nothing's working

Four beliefs required for change

Build and maintain a true leadership team. Accept that hitting the ceiling is inevitable. Run the business on one operating system. Be open-minded, growth-oriented and vulnerable.

Put it into practice

Ask your leadership team which of the five frustrations is most expensive today. Then identify the EOS component most likely to expose its root cause.

02 / People

Right people. Right seats.

RIGHT PEOPLE + RIGHT SEATS = THE PEOPLE YOU NEED

Two distinct tests

Right people: Fit the core values, assessed with the People Analyzer.

Right seats: Pass GWC: Get it, Want it, Capacity to do it. Capacity includes time, mental, physical and emotional ability.

Put it into practice

Review the chart: Is this the right structure for the next level? Are the right people in the right seats? Does everyone have enough time to do the job well? Address people issues with explicit expectations and follow-up.

04 / Issues

Identify. Discuss. Solve.

The point is to address root causes, not spend a meeting circling symptoms. Keep three issues lists: longer-term V/TO, weekly leadership, and departmental items requiring attention this week.

I · Identify

Choose the top three issues. Find the root cause. Distinguish a true problem, information to communicate or an idea needing input.

D · Discuss

Get facts, options, concerns and ideas into the open. Capture tangents on the issues list rather than following them.

S · Solve

Assign a To-Do, confirm shared understanding, or assign research to a specific person.

The successful decision habit

Reach decisions promptly. Change decisions slowly. Consensus is not required; firsthand facts, decisiveness and the greater good matter.

Accept the discomfort

Live with it, end it or change it. Choose short-term discomfort when necessary to resolve the real issue.

Put it into practice

At the next leadership meeting, rank the top three issues, use IDS on the first one, and end with an explicit owner and outcome.

05 / Process

Document your way. Follow it consistently.

Systemizing the core processes creates consistency, scalability and a more valuable business that can grow, sell, duplicate or maintain.

1 · Document

Name the core processes. Accountable owners capture the high-level 20% of steps producing 80% of results; simplify and add checklists.

2 · Package

Give each process a name and combine them in one accessible manual: “[Your Company] Way.”

3 · Follow

Create a visual, share it company-wide, retrain everyone and manage adherence, including owners.

Typical core processes

HR · Marketing · Sales · Operations (often one to three) · Accounting · Customer retention

Why it matters

A repeatable, turnkey operation is easier to grow, duplicate, maintain or sell.

Put it into practice

Schedule a one-hour leadership meeting to name the core processes. Assign one accountable documenter to each. Start with the highest-impact process.

06 / Traction

Quarterly Rocks. A consistent meeting pulse.

Quarterly Rocks

List 10–20 candidate priorities, debate the top 3–7, define measurable outcomes and due dates, and assign exactly one owner to each. Share company Rocks; cascade to leaders, departments and individuals. Employees typically have 1–3.

Meeting pulse

Weekly, quarterly and annually. Same day, same time, same agenda, on time. Build the habit with the leadership team before rolling it out.

“Your meetings should be passionate, intense, exhausting, and never boring.” — Patrick Lencioni, quoted in the original summary

Make the pulse predictable

Same day, same time, same agenda, on time. Consistency is the operating discipline, not a scheduling preference.

Keep issues in their place

Off-track metrics, Rocks and To-Dos feed the issues list. Don't consume the review sections debating them.

End with accountability

Decisions, owners and To-Dos must be explicit. Confirm what needs communicating and rate the meeting.

Put it into practice

Write each Rock as a specific outcome, due date and single owner. Review its on-track/off-track status weekly. Avoid adding new priorities mid-quarter.
